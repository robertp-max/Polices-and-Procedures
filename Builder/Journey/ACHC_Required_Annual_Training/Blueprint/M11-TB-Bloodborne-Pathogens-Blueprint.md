## 1. Module Overview

Blood borne pathogens (HBV, HCV, HIV) and tuberculosis represent the most significant occupational exposure risks for home health field workers. Unlike hospital settings, home environments lack engineered controls, negative pressure rooms, and immediate post-exposure resources. This module ensures clinicians understand transmission pathways, Standard Precautions, respiratory protection requirements, PPD testing protocols, exposure response procedures, and the Agency's TB risk classification system.

---

## 2. Pre-Assessment Hook (Scenario-Based)

**Scenario:** You arrive at a patient's home for a routine skilled nursing visit. The patient has a persistent productive cough (3+ weeks), reports night sweats, unintentional weight loss, and fatigue. They tell you: "My doctor thinks it's just a cold that won't go away." The patient is not on any respiratory isolation. Your last PPD was negative 6 months ago. The patient coughs directly in your direction while you're taking vital signs.

**Question:** What is your IMMEDIATE concern and action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Continue the visit — the doctor said it's just a cold |
| B | This presentation is consistent with active pulmonary TB. Don your N95 respirator immediately, complete only essential assessment, report findings to supervisor AND the patient's physician immediately for TB workup, and document the potential exposure |
| C | Apply a surgical mask and continue — it provides enough protection |
| D | Leave immediately without providing any care |

**Correct:** B  
**Hook Purpose:** Tests whether learners can recognize TB symptom clusters and take immediate protective action in the field without waiting for a confirmed diagnosis.

## 3. Learning Objectives

Upon completion, the learner will be able to:

1. Define blood borne pathogens and identify the primary transmission routes.
2. Apply Standard Precautions and engineering controls to prevent exposure.
3. Execute the immediate post-exposure response protocol for needlesticks and splash exposures.
4. Recognize the signs and symptoms of active pulmonary tuberculosis.
5. Describe the Agency's TB risk classification system and PPD testing requirements.
6. Demonstrate proper use and fit-check of N95 respiratory protection.

## 4. Core Concepts (Terminology) — Max 7

| # | Term | Definition | Operational Meaning | Why It Matters |
|---|------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Blood Borne Pathogens | Infectious microorganisms in human blood (HBV, HCV, HIV) that cause disease | Transmitted through direct/indirect contact, needlesticks, mucous membrane exposure | #1 occupational infection risk for healthcare workers |
| 2 | Exposure Control Plan | OSHA-required written program for eliminating/minimizing employee exposure | Must be available, known, and followed by ALL employees | Defines your protection AND your post-exposure rights |
| 3 | Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) | Medical treatment given after potential exposure to prevent infection | Must begin within hours (HBV) — delays reduce effectiveness dramatically | Time-critical: report immediately, not "later" |
| 4 | N95 Respirator | NIOSH-approved particulate respirator; minimum protection for TB exposure | Must be fit-tested before use; reusable if structural integrity maintained | Surgical masks are NOT adequate for airborne TB |
| 5 | Mantoux PPD (2-Step) | Tuberculin skin test used to detect TB infection | Required at hire; annual questionnaire thereafter (if negative) | Conversion = chest X-ray required; positive + symptoms = work exclusion |
| 6 | TB Risk Classification | Agency's risk level (Very Low/Low/Intermediate/High) based on patient population and conversion rates | Determines testing frequency and policy requirements | Annual reassessment required as part of infection control program |
| 7 | Engineering Controls | Primary means of eliminating exposure (needleless devices, shielded needles, sharps containers) | Must be used BEFORE relying on PPE or work practice controls | OSHA hierarchy: engineering > work practice > PPE |

## 5. Lesson Structure

### Lesson 1: Blood Borne Pathogens — Transmission & Prevention

**Content Coverage:**
- BBP definition: HBV, HCV, HIV (primary pathogens)
- Transmission routes: direct contact, indirect contact, respiratory droplet, vector-borne
- Standard Precautions: treat ALL blood/body fluids as infectious
- Engineering controls: safer medical devices, needleless systems, shielded needles
- Work practice controls: biohazard bags, sharps containers, no recapping needles
- PPE: gloves, gowns, masks, eye protection, CPR barriers
- Decontamination: 1:10 bleach solution, 10 minutes contact time
- Hepatitis B vaccination: must be offered within 10 working days of initial assignment

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Engineering controls are the PRIMARY defense (not PPE, not "being careful")
- HBV vaccination: offered within 10 days of hire; employee may decline but employer must offer later if they change their mind
- All body fluids treated as infectious — don't wait for diagnosis
- Remove jewelry before gloving; cover cuts/scrapes; change gloves between patients
- Biohazard labels required on ALL containers of contaminated materials

---

### Challenge 1: Exposure Prevention Decision

**Scenario:** You are providing wound care to a patient with a heavily draining abdominal wound. The drainage is blood-tinged and you need to irrigate the wound, which typically produces splash. You have gloves and a gown available but no face shield. The patient's HIV status is unknown.

**Question:** What is the CORRECT action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Proceed with gloves and gown only — the splash risk is minimal if you're careful |
| B | Do not perform the irrigation without appropriate face/eye protection; irrigating wounds produces splash and blood-tinged drainage requires full Standard Precautions including eye protection |
| C | Proceed but turn your head away during irrigation |
| D | Ask the patient their HIV status to determine if you need face protection |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** Standard Precautions require protection based on ANTICIPATED EXPOSURE. Wound irrigation with blood-tinged drainage = splash risk = face/eye protection required. Patient HIV status is irrelevant — all body fluids are treated as infectious.

---

### Operational Debrief 1

- **Why B is correct:** You must have appropriate PPE BEFORE beginning a procedure with splash risk. Missing equipment = reschedule or obtain equipment first. Patient safety includes provider safety.
- **Why A violates Standard Precautions:** "Minimal splash risk" is subjective. The ANTICIPATED exposure determines PPE, not your optimistic estimate.
- **Why C is inadequate:** "Turning your head" is not an engineering or administrative control — it's hope. Splash travels unpredictably.
- **Why D violates Standard Precautions fundamentally:** Standard Precautions means ALL body fluids treated as infectious regardless of known status. You never base PPE decisions on patient diagnosis.
- **Practical Resolution:** Either obtain face protection (should be in your nursing bag) or defer the irrigation to next visit with proper supplies.
- **OSHA Requirement:** Employer must provide PPE. If it's not in your bag, that's a supply issue to report — not a reason to proceed without protection.

### Lesson 2: Post-Exposure Response

**Content Coverage:**
- Immediate response: wash needlestick/cuts with soap and water; flush eyes/nose/mouth with water/saline for 20 minutes
- Reporting: immediately to appropriate person per exposure control plan + to EMS if they take over care
- Documentation: date, time, circumstances, actions taken, information required by employer
- Source individual testing: consent sought; if already known positive, testing not repeated
- Employee blood collection: as soon as feasible; can preserve sample 90 days if HIV test declined initially
- Post-exposure prophylaxis: when medically indicated per U.S. Public Health Service standards
- Physician evaluation: must receive OSHA regulation copy, exposure description, source results, employee medical records
- Written opinion within 15 days: limited to notification of results, conditions requiring treatment; all other findings confidential

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- WASH IMMEDIATELY — soap and water for skin; irrigate eyes 20 minutes with clean water/saline
- Report IMMEDIATELY — time sensitivity is critical for prophylaxis effectiveness
- Employee can decline HIV testing initially but sample preserved 90 days if they change their mind
- Employer must provide written physician opinion within 15 days
- All findings beyond notification remain CONFIDENTIAL — not disclosed to employer

### Challenge 2: Post-Exposure Sequence

**Scenario:** While drawing blood, the patient moves unexpectedly and the needle punctures through your glove into your thumb. You see blood. The patient tells you they are Hepatitis C positive.

**Question:** Arrange the CORRECT post-exposure actions in proper sequence:

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Report → Test → Wash → Document → Follow-up |
| B | Wash immediately with soap and water → Report to supervisor per exposure control plan → Document the incident (date, time, circumstances) → Seek medical evaluation → Follow up per physician recommendations |
| C | Finish the blood draw → Bandage your thumb → Report at end of shift |
| D | Ask the patient more questions about their Hep C status → Decide if reporting is necessary |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** The sequence is: immediate wound decontamination → immediate reporting → documentation → medical evaluation → follow-up. Patient's known Hep C status makes this MORE urgent, not less.

---

### Operational Debrief 2

- **Why B is correct:** Follows OSHA-mandated post-exposure protocol exactly. Washing must happen FIRST (within seconds, not minutes). Everything else flows from that.
- **Why A is wrong sequence:** Reporting before washing wastes critical decontamination time.
- **Why C is negligent:** Never finish the procedure before addressing your exposure. And NEVER delay reporting to end of shift — prophylaxis effectiveness is time-dependent.
- **Why D is irrelevant:** The patient has CONFIRMED Hep C status — no additional questions are needed, and known status makes immediate action MORE critical.
- **HCV-Specific:** Currently no PEP for HCV (unlike HIV and HBV), but immediate medical evaluation, baseline testing, and monitoring schedule are still mandatory.
- **Employee Rights:** Testing results and follow-up remain confidential. Employer receives only that you were evaluated and any conditions requiring treatment — not specific results.

### Lesson 3: Tuberculosis — Recognition, Protection & Policy

**Content Coverage:**
- TB symptoms: productive cough, hemoptysis, weight loss, loss of appetite, lethargy, weakness, night sweats, fever
- TB transmission: airborne; risk in home care during cough-producing procedures
- Agency policy: does not admit TB patients; refers to collaborative agencies
- Risk classification system: Very Low, Minimal, Low, Intermediate, High (with definitions)
- PPD testing requirements: 2-step Mantoux on hire (or negative within 12 months); annual questionnaire
- Positive conversion protocol: chest X-ray, medical evaluation, annual TB questionnaire (no repeat X-ray per CDC)
- Staff with active TB: excluded until 3 consecutive negative AFB smears + physician clearance
- Respiratory protection: N95 minimum; fit-tested before use; reusable if intact
- Home care specifics: no negative pressure rooms → well-ventilated areas, open windows, collect specimens outdoors

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- TB is AIRBORNE — a surgical mask does NOT protect you (N95 required)
- Agency does NOT admit TB patients — but if discovered during care, continue with protections until transfer or non-infectious status
- N95 must be fit-tested PRIOR to use — one-size-does-NOT-fit-all
- Staff who cannot be fit-tested or cannot wear N95 → NOT assigned to suspected/confirmed TB patients
- Cough-producing procedures in homes: do in well-ventilated area, away from household members, consider outdoors
- Positive PPD conversion → chest X-ray → annual questionnaire only (no repeat X-ray per CDC guidelines)

### Challenge 3: TB Recognition & Response

**Scenario:** Your assigned patient (no TB history) has developed a persistent cough over the past month. Today you note: productive cough with blood-tinged sputum, the patient reports night sweats for 2 weeks, and has visibly lost weight since your last visit. The patient is a 68-year-old immigrant from the Philippines (high-risk population). They have no respiratory isolation orders.

**Question:** What actions are REQUIRED? (Select ALL that apply)

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Don your N95 respirator immediately |
| B | Report findings to the patient's physician immediately for TB workup |
| C | Notify your supervisor (DON) of suspected TB |
| D | Report to the local health department |
| E | Continue routine care with just a surgical mask since TB isn't confirmed |

**Correct:** A, B, C, D  
**Rationale:** This symptom cluster in a high-risk population = suspected TB until proven otherwise. Respiratory protection (N95), physician notification, supervisor notification, AND health department reporting are ALL required for suspected TB.

---

### Operational Debrief 3

- **Why A is immediately required:** You are in an enclosed space with a patient showing classic TB symptoms. N95 is the minimum respiratory protection for suspected TB.
- **Why B is critical:** Physician must order TB workup (sputum AFB smear, culture, chest X-ray). You cannot diagnose, but you can and must report the clinical findings.
- **Why C is required by policy:** DON/QI Director must be notified of suspected TB for infection control program management.
- **Why D is required by law:** Suspected TB triggers health department notification — not just confirmed cases.
- **Why E is dangerous:** Surgical masks do NOT protect against airborne TB. They filter large droplets only — TB is transmitted via droplet nuclei that pass through surgical masks.
- **High-Risk Populations:** Philippines is a high-TB-prevalence country; combined with symptom cluster = very high clinical suspicion.
- **Your Follow-Up:** After leaving the home, document your potential exposure and notify infection control for PPD monitoring.

## 6. Summary & Synthesis

**Key Takeaways:**
1. Standard Precautions: treat ALL body fluids as infectious, ALWAYS, regardless of diagnosis.
2. Engineering controls > Work practice controls > PPE — this is the OSHA hierarchy.
3. Post-exposure: WASH IMMEDIATELY → Report immediately → Medical evaluation → Documentation. Time is critical.
4. TB is AIRBORNE — surgical masks are inadequate. N95 fit-tested respirator is the minimum.
5. Suspected TB = act as if confirmed until proven otherwise. Don't wait for lab results to protect yourself.
6. HBV vaccination must be offered within 10 days of hire — your right as an employee.

**Operational Bridge:** Your field preceptor will evaluate your ability to demonstrate N95 fit-check, articulate the post-exposure protocol, recognize TB symptoms, and execute the Exposure Control Plan.
