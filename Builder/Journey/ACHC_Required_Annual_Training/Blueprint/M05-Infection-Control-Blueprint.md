## 1. Module Overview

Home health clinicians deliver care in uncontrolled environments where plumbing, sanitation, and ventilation may be poor or absent. Standard Precautions apply to ALL patients regardless of diagnosis. This module ensures field workers can execute proper hand hygiene, PPE selection, bag technique, sharps handling, specimen transport, and reportable disease notification in the home setting.

---

## 2. Pre-Assessment Hook (Scenario-Based)

**Scenario:** You arrive at a patient's home for wound care. The home has no running water — the pipes froze overnight. The patient's wound dressing is saturated and malodorous. You have your nursing bag, hand sanitizer, and gloves. The patient says: "Just change it quick — I know you're busy."

**Question:** What is your CORRECT action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Use hand sanitizer, don gloves, and proceed with wound care — no water isn't ideal but the wound needs attention |
| B | Assess the wound urgency, use alcohol-based hand sanitizer (acceptable when hands are not visibly soiled), don gloves, perform the dressing change with aseptic technique, and document the environmental limitation |
| C | Refuse to provide care until running water is restored — infection control requires soap and water |
| D | Use the patient's bottled drinking water to wash hands and proceed |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** CDC/Agency guidelines state alcohol-based sanitizer IS acceptable when hands are not visibly soiled. Patient care cannot be abandoned due to environmental limitations, but those limitations must be documented and reported.

---

## 3. Learning Objectives

Upon completion, the learner will be able to:

1. Apply Standard Precautions for all patient encounters regardless of diagnosis.
2. Demonstrate correct hand hygiene technique (including when alcohol-based sanitizer is and is not acceptable).
3. Select appropriate PPE based on anticipated exposure (gloves, gowns, goggles, masks).
4. Execute proper bag technique to minimize cross-contamination.
5. Handle sharps safely and describe the Agency's exposure control plan.
6. Identify reportable diseases and the notification process.

---

## 4. Core Concepts (Terminology) — Max 7

| # | Term | Definition | Operational Meaning | Why It Matters |
|---|------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Standard Precautions | Infection control system based on degree of EXPOSURE, not diagnosis | Treat ALL blood/body fluids as potentially infectious for ALL patients | Replaces diagnosis-based isolation; eliminates guessing |
| 2 | Alcohol-Based Hand Rub | Preferred hand hygiene method when hands are not visibly soiled | Acceptable for routine decontamination in all clinical situations | Faster, more accessible, more effective than soap for routine use |
| 3 | Bag Technique | Protocol for carrying/using nursing supplies in patient homes | Bag never on floor; barrier used; hands cleansed before entering bag | Prevents cross-contamination between patients |
| 4 | Exposure Control Plan | Written program outlining measures to eliminate/minimize employee exposure | Required by OSHA; must be available and known to all employees | Defines what to do WHEN (not if) exposure occurs |
| 5 | Reportable Disease | Infections requiring notification to public health department per state law | Clinician identifies → reports to DON/QI Director → form to health department | Legal obligation; failure to report = violation |
| 6 | Sharps Container | Puncture-resistant container for used needles and sharp instruments | Fill to 3/4 only; never recap needles; dispose per regulations | Needlesticks are #1 cause of bloodborne pathogen exposure |
| 7 | Aseptic Technique | Methods used to prevent contamination during sterile procedures | Required for wound care, catheter insertion, IV procedures | Breach of technique = potential patient infection |

## 5. Lesson Structure

### Lesson 1: Standard Precautions in the Home Setting

**Content Coverage:**
- Standard Precautions philosophy: exposure-based, not diagnosis-based
- Hand washing requirements: before/after ALL patient contact, after glove removal, between contaminated and clean areas
- Glove requirements: blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin
- Goggle/face shield: splash potential (venipuncture, catheter insertion, suctioning)
- Gowns/Aprons: splatter/spray potential
- Mask usage: respiratory precautions, splash risk
- Needle safety: never recap, bend, or break; straight into sharps container
- Contamination response: immediate soap and water wash

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Assume ALL blood and body fluids are infectious — for EVERY patient
- Gloves don't replace hand washing — wash AFTER removing gloves
- Change gloves between EACH patient contact
- Health care workers with exudative lesions/weeping dermatitis must refrain from direct patient care

---

### Challenge 1: PPE Selection Scenario

**Scenario:** You are about to perform a tracheostomy suctioning procedure on a patient. The patient has a productive cough and has been expectorating blood-tinged sputum. The patient's TB test from last month was negative.

**Question:** What PPE is required for this procedure?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Gloves only — TB is negative so respiratory protection isn't needed |
| B | Gloves and gown only |
| C | Gloves, gown, goggles/face shield, and mask — suctioning produces splash/aerosol with blood-tinged secretions |
| D | No PPE needed — the patient isn't on isolation precautions |

**Correct:** C  
**Rationale:** Standard Precautions are based on EXPOSURE risk, not diagnosis. Suctioning produces aerosol + blood-tinged secretions = splash/splatter risk requiring full face protection regardless of TB status.

---

### Operational Debrief 1

- **Why C is correct:** Suctioning = aerosol-generating procedure. Blood-tinged sputum = body fluid exposure. Standard Precautions require protection based on what you'll be EXPOSED to, not the patient's diagnosis.
- **Why A is dangerous:** Negative TB doesn't eliminate all respiratory pathogens. More importantly, blood-tinged aerosol requires face/eye protection regardless of TB status.
- **Why B is incomplete:** Gown protects clothing but doesn't protect eyes, nose, or mouth from aerosol exposure.
- **Why D is fundamentally wrong:** Standard Precautions apply to ALL patients — "no isolation precautions" doesn't mean "no precautions."
- **Patient Safety Impact:** Clinician infection → absent from work → patients miss visits → care gaps.
- **Survey Implication:** ACHC observes PPE practices during surveys; incorrect selection = immediate finding.

---

### Lesson 2: Hand Hygiene & Bag Technique

**Content Coverage:**
- Hand hygiene policy: alcohol-based sanitizer preferred (until dry); antimicrobial soap ≥15 seconds
- 11 specific situations requiring hand hygiene
- When soap + water is REQUIRED over sanitizer (visibly soiled, C-Diff, proteinaceous material)
- Nail length (<1/4 inch), artificial nails prohibited, rings discouraged
- Bag technique: never on floor, barrier required, zip closed, orderly supplies
- Virulent organism/vermin protocol: double plastic bag method
- Equipment cleaning between patients
- Monthly bag deep-clean requirement

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Alcohol-based sanitizer is PREFERRED for routine decontamination (not just acceptable)
- C-Diff requires soap and water specifically (CDC recommendation B-III)
- Bag goes on shoulder until barrier is placed — never set on unprotected surface
- Hands cleansed BEFORE entering/removing items from bag — every single time
- Thermometer covers always used; thermometer disinfected between patients

### Challenge 2: Hand Hygiene Decision

**Scenario:** You've just removed your gloves after providing perineal care to a patient with known C-Diff infection. You used the double-bag technique for your supplies. Your hands are not visibly soiled. You need to retrieve your stethoscope from your nursing bag for a quick vital signs check.

**Question:** What hand hygiene method is required BEFORE accessing your bag?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Alcohol-based hand sanitizer — hands aren't visibly soiled and it's faster |
| B | Soap and water — C-Diff specifically requires soap/water even when hands appear clean |
| C | Just change to new gloves — that's sufficient barrier protection |
| D | Antimicrobial wipes — they're acceptable as an alternative |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** CDC specifically recommends soap and water (not alcohol-based products) for C-Diff during outbreaks or increased rates. Alcohol does not effectively kill C-Diff spores.

---

### Operational Debrief 2

- **Why B is correct:** C-Diff is the specific exception to the alcohol-based sanitizer preference. The organism forms spores that alcohol cannot kill. CDC rates this B-III evidence.
- **Why A is incorrect for this specific pathogen:** While normally preferred, alcohol-based products are specifically NOT recommended for C-Diff. This is a critical operational distinction.
- **Why C is wrong:** Gloves don't replace hand washing. You must wash before accessing clean supplies regardless.
- **Why D is insufficient:** Antimicrobial wipes are "not as effective" as alcohol products — which themselves are insufficient for C-Diff.
- **Workflow Impact:** After C-Diff care, locate soap/water source BEFORE touching anything else. Plan ahead.
- **Patient Safety Impact:** Cross-contaminating your bag with C-Diff spores → transmission to every subsequent patient.

---

### Lesson 3: Sharps Safety, Specimens & Reportable Diseases

**Content Coverage:**
- Needle safety: never recap, bend, break, or manipulate
- Sharps container: puncture-resistant, return to Agency at 3/4 full
- Specimen handling: treat ALL as contaminated, sealable bags, secured transport
- Soiled linen handling: minimal agitation, leak-proof bags
- Spill cleanup: gloves, disinfectant-detergent, physical scrubbing
- 1:10 bleach solution for blood contamination
- Reportable diseases: list, process, forms, log
- Reporting chain: field staff → DON/QI Director → health department

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- NEVER recap a needle — this is the #1 cause of needlestick injuries
- Sharps container at 3/4 = STOP using it, return for new one
- All specimens labeled, bagged, and transported in secured containers
- Reportable diseases: staff identifies → DON confirms via record review → form to health department
- Physician notified; log maintained; compliance with current state regulations
- Employees prohibited from eating/drinking where patient care or lab testing is performed

### Challenge 3: Needlestick Exposure

**Scenario:** While removing an IV catheter, the needle slips and punctures your glove and finger. You see blood. The patient's HIV and Hepatitis status is unknown.

**Question:** What are your IMMEDIATE actions in correct order?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Finish the procedure, bandage your finger, note it in your day planner to report tomorrow |
| B | Stop the procedure safely, wash the injury with soap and water immediately, report to supervisor as per exposure control plan, seek medical evaluation, document the incident |
| C | Squeeze the wound to push out any contaminated blood, apply alcohol, and continue the visit |
| D | Ask the patient if they have any bloodborne diseases and decide whether to report based on their answer |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** OSHA's Exposure Control Plan requires immediate wound washing, immediate reporting, medical evaluation, and documentation. Patient status is irrelevant to the requirement to report.

---

### Operational Debrief 3

- **Why B is correct:** Follows the OSHA-mandated post-exposure sequence exactly. Time is critical — prophylaxis effectiveness decreases with delay.
- **Why A is dangerous:** Delayed reporting reduces effectiveness of post-exposure prophylaxis. Hepatitis B PEP must begin within hours, not days.
- **Why C is incorrect technique:** Squeezing the wound is NOT recommended (may increase tissue penetration). Also, continuing the visit without reporting violates the exposure control plan.
- **Why D is irrelevant:** You don't need to know patient status to be required to report. Universal precautions assume ALL exposures are potentially infectious.
- **Documentation Impact:** Must record date, time, circumstances, actions taken, and follow-up per employer's plan.
- **Legal Protection:** HBV vaccination must be offered within 10 working days of initial assignment. Employee can decline but employer must make it available later if requested.

## 6. Summary & Synthesis

**Key Takeaways:**
1. Standard Precautions = based on EXPOSURE, not diagnosis. Apply to ALL patients, ALL encounters.
2. Alcohol-based sanitizer is PREFERRED for routine use — EXCEPT C-Diff (soap + water required).
3. Bag technique: never on floor, barrier always, clean hands before entering, monthly deep clean.
4. NEVER recap needles. Sharps container at 3/4 full = get a new one.
5. Needlestick exposure: wash immediately → report immediately → medical evaluation → document.
6. Reportable diseases are LEGAL obligations — report through DON to health department.

**Operational Bridge:** Your field preceptor will observe your hand hygiene technique, PPE selection, bag technique execution, and sharps handling during home visits.

- [ ] A. Report to your supervisor

**Q6.** A sharps container should be returned for disposal when it is:

## 8. Mandatory Final Operational Debrief

**Reflection Prompt:** "A correct answer does NOT guarantee correct reasoning."

1. Standard Precautions are NON-NEGOTIABLE and diagnosis-INDEPENDENT — no exceptions.
2. The home environment creates unique infection risks that don't exist in facilities — your bag technique is your primary defense.
3. C-Diff is the critical exception to alcohol-based sanitizer — memorize this distinction.
4. Needlestick response is TIME-SENSITIVE — delayed reporting reduces prophylaxis effectiveness.
5. Cross-contamination between patients via your supplies/hands is YOUR personal responsibility.
