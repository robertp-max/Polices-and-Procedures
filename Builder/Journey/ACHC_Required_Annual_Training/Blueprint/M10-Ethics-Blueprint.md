## 1. Module Overview

Ethical decision-making in healthcare goes beyond legal compliance — it requires navigating complex situations where multiple valid perspectives conflict. Field workers face ethical dilemmas daily: advance directives vs. family wishes, patient autonomy vs. safety, religious practices vs. medical need, and professional boundaries vs. compassion. This module develops the ethical reasoning framework needed for defensible clinical decisions.

---

## 2. Pre-Assessment Hook (Scenario-Based)

**Scenario:** You are caring for a terminally ill patient with a valid Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) order. During your visit, the patient goes into cardiac arrest. The patient's adult son arrives, sees you not performing CPR, and screams: "WHAT ARE YOU DOING? SAVE HIM! I don't care about that paper — he's my FATHER!" He begins pushing you toward the patient.

**Question:** What is the ETHICALLY and LEGALLY correct action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Begin CPR — the family member's distress overrides the DNR in the moment |
| B | Honor the DNR order as a legally binding document; calmly explain the patient's wishes while ensuring your physical safety; contact the supervisor/physician; support the family member's grief without overriding the patient's documented directive |
| C | Leave immediately — this is above your pay grade |
| D | Ask the son to sign something saying he overrides the DNR, then begin CPR |

**Correct:** B  
**Hook Purpose:** Exposes the emotional complexity of ethical situations where legal/ethical correctness conflicts with human compassion. Tests whether learners can maintain ethical boundaries under extreme emotional pressure.

## 3. Learning Objectives

Upon completion, the learner will be able to:

1. Define healthcare ethics and distinguish ethical obligations from legal requirements.
2. Identify the major categories of ethical issues in home health (confidentiality, consent, advance directives, relationships, discrimination).
3. Apply the Agency's Code of Ethics across its 5 domains (Patient Rights, Provider Relations, Fiscal, Marketing, Personnel).
4. Describe the Ethics Committee's role and how to initiate ethical consultation.
5. Navigate situations where personal values conflict with professional duty.
6. Document ethical decisions and their rationale defensibly.

## 4. Core Concepts (Terminology) — Max 7

| # | Term | Definition | Operational Meaning | Why It Matters |
|---|------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Healthcare Ethics | Making decisions that consider patient beliefs, wishes, and welfare alongside medical best practices | Framework for navigating gray areas where law alone doesn't provide answers | Ethical failures → complaints, lawsuits, loss of licensure |
| 2 | Ethics Committee | Ad Hoc committee of Professional Advisory Committee convened for ethical dilemmas | Available for consultation; includes Medical Director and involved staff | Provides defensible framework for difficult decisions |
| 3 | Advance Directive | Legal document expressing patient's end-of-life care wishes (DNR, living will, healthcare proxy) | MUST be honored by the Agency; check for existence at admission | Overriding a valid advance directive = assault/battery |
| 4 | Patient Autonomy | The patient's right to make their own healthcare decisions, even "bad" ones | Cannot force treatment on a competent patient even to save their life | Fundamental ethical principle; overridden only by court order |
| 5 | Informed Consent | Patient's right to complete information before agreeing to treatment | Must include risks, benefits, alternatives, consequences of refusal | Treatment without informed consent = legally battery |
| 6 | Professional Boundaries | Limits of the therapeutic relationship between clinician and patient | No sexual relationships, no financial entanglement, no dual relationships | Violations = termination, loss of license, criminal charges |
| 7 | Conscientious Objection | Staff right to decline participation in care that conflicts with personal values/beliefs | Must not disrupt patient care; alternative arrangements required | Protected IF patient care is maintained by alternative means |

## 5. Lesson Structure

### Lesson 1: Ethical Framework & Major Issues

**Content Coverage:**
- Healthcare ethics definition and scope
- Ethical vs. legal (legal ≠ ethical; ethical ≠ legal)
- Major ethical issues: confidentiality, disease transmission risk, aggressive marketing, information accuracy, patient welfare, elderly patients, terminally ill patients, sexual harassment, therapies, relationships, malpractice, consent, discrimination, honesty
- The Agency's Code of Ethics: 5 domains (Patient Rights, Provider Relations, Fiscal, Marketing, Personnel)
- Ethics Committee: composition, convening, role of Medical Director

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Something can be LEGAL but not ETHICAL (performing an abortion) or ETHICAL but not LEGAL (circumstances vary)
- Healthcare professionals face ethical decisions DAILY, not just in dramatic end-of-life situations
- Confidentiality is BOTH an ethical AND legal obligation
- Patient welfare is the PRIMARY motive — personal beliefs do not override
- The Ethics Committee is available for ANY ethical dilemma — you don't need to solve it alone

### Challenge 1: Confidentiality vs. Safety

**Scenario:** Your patient confides: "My husband has been hitting me, but if you tell anyone, he'll kill me. He checks my phone and monitors my appointments. Please just treat my injuries and don't say anything." You observe injuries consistent with intimate partner violence. The patient is competent and is explicitly requesting confidentiality.

**Question:** What is the ETHICAL course of action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Respect confidentiality completely — the patient is competent and made a clear request |
| B | Report to law enforcement immediately — domestic violence must always be reported |
| C | Document injuries objectively, provide safety resources discreetly, respect the patient's autonomy while assessing immediate danger level, consult with your supervisor about mandatory reporting requirements in your state, and safety plan WITH the patient |
| D | Refuse to provide care until the patient agrees to report |

**Correct:** C  
**Rationale:** This is a genuine ethical dilemma where multiple principles conflict (autonomy vs. safety, confidentiality vs. harm prevention). The answer is not a simple binary — it requires nuanced action that respects autonomy while fulfilling professional obligations.

---

### Operational Debrief 1

- **Why C is correct:** Ethical action here means honoring autonomy while not abandoning your professional duty. Document objectively, provide resources, consult supervision, and determine whether state law MANDATES reporting (varies by state for adult competent patients).
- **Why A may be insufficient:** While patient autonomy is paramount, if your state has mandatory reporting for domestic violence against vulnerable adults OR if the patient's life is in imminent danger, confidentiality has limits.
- **Why B may be premature:** Not all states mandate reporting of domestic violence against competent adults. Reporting without patient consent may actually increase danger if the abuser discovers it. State-specific law governs.
- **Why D abandons the patient:** Conditioning care on reporting = coercion. You don't withhold treatment to force compliance with your preferred action.
- **Ethics Committee Role:** This is EXACTLY the type of situation for Ethics Committee consultation. You are not expected to resolve this alone.
- **Documentation:** Objective injury description. NEVER document "domestic violence" if the patient hasn't confirmed (puts patient at risk if records are accessed by abuser).

### Lesson 2: Advance Directives & End-of-Life Ethics

**Content Coverage:**
- Advance Directive types: DNR, Do Not Intubate, Living Will, Healthcare Proxy/Power of Attorney
- Agency obligation to honor advance directives (42 CFR 489)
- When family conflicts with patient's documented wishes
- Physician non-responsiveness to nurse concerns
- Patient refusal of part of ordered care (but acceptance of another part)
- Elderly patients: decision-making capacity ≠ age; legal incompetence requires court determination
- Terminally ill patients: euthanasia, comfort care, quality of life decisions
- The Ethics Committee's role in end-of-life decisions

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Advance directives are LEGALLY BINDING — family members cannot override them verbally
- "Living will" should be checked for at admission — ask about its existence
- Family conflict about advance directives → Ethics Committee consultation
- Patient can refuse PART of care while accepting another part — this is their right
- Only a COURT can determine legal incompetence — age/diagnosis alone is not sufficient

### Challenge 2: Family vs. Patient Wishes

**Scenario:** Your patient is 88 years old with advanced COPD. He has a valid living will stating "no intubation, no mechanical ventilation." His daughter (healthcare proxy) calls you and says: "I've changed my mind. If Dad can't breathe, I want him on a ventilator. I'm his proxy — I have the authority."

**Question:** What is the CORRECT understanding of this situation?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | The daughter is correct — as healthcare proxy, she can override the living will at any time |
| B | The healthcare proxy's authority activates when the patient CANNOT speak for himself; while the patient is competent, HIS living will represents HIS wishes. If he becomes incapacitated, the proxy must act in accordance with his known wishes, not substitute their own preferences |
| C | Call the physician and let them decide — this is above your scope |
| D | Honor whichever document is more recent |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** A healthcare proxy's purpose is to ensure the PATIENT'S wishes are followed when the patient can't speak, NOT to substitute the proxy's own preferences. The living will is the patient's documented wish.

---

### Operational Debrief 2

- **Why B is correct:** The proxy's role is to represent the patient, not themselves. A proxy who acts contrary to the patient's documented wishes may be acting outside their authority. While competent, the patient's own directive governs.
- **Why A is incorrect:** Proxy authority has LIMITS. A proxy cannot override a clear directive that represents the patient's known wishes simply because they disagree. Their role is to speak FOR the patient, not OVER the patient.
- **Why C is incomplete:** While physician involvement is appropriate, the nurse should understand the ethical framework to explain the situation accurately and advocate for the patient's documented wishes.
- **Why D is wrong:** "More recent" is not the standard. The patient's own expressed wishes while competent take precedence over proxy preferences.
- **Escalation Path:** Ethics Committee + Attending Physician + Legal counsel if proxy insists on overriding documented patient wishes.
- **Documentation:** Document the conflict, note the patient's directive, note the proxy's request, and document that consultation was sought.

---

### Lesson 3: Staff Ethical Conflicts & Professional Boundaries

**Content Coverage:**
- Staff personal values vs. professional duty
- Examples: religious holidays, right-to-life issues, blood transfusions, refusing care due to beliefs, sexual orientation
- Policy: staff refusal based on values must NOT disrupt patient care
- Alternative care arrangements required
- Performance evaluations must consider religious/cultural motives
- Professional boundaries: no sexual relationships, no financial entanglement
- Malpractice: defensive medicine, duty of care
- Discrimination: zero tolerance regardless of source

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- You CAN decline participation in specific care based on personal beliefs — but patient care must NOT be disrupted
- The Agency must have an alternative method when staff decline based on values
- Performance evaluations must NOT penalize value-based conscientious objection (when properly handled)
- Sexual relationships with patients are ALWAYS forbidden — no exceptions, no "after discharge" loopholes
- Malpractice: act within scope, document thoroughly, report errors honestly

### Challenge 3: Personal Values Conflict

**Scenario:** You are assigned to a patient who is in a same-sex marriage. Your religious beliefs consider homosexuality sinful. The patient requires routine wound care — there is nothing about the care itself that conflicts with your beliefs, only the patient's identity/lifestyle.

**Question:** What is the ETHICALLY and PROFESSIONALLY correct response?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Request reassignment — your religious beliefs give you the right to refuse |
| B | Provide the care professionally without discrimination; your personal beliefs about a patient's identity do not constitute grounds for conscientious objection to routine clinical care |
| C | Provide care but share your religious perspective with the patient |
| D | Provide care but document that you object to the patient's lifestyle |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** Conscientious objection applies to SPECIFIC PROCEDURES that conflict with beliefs (e.g., administering blood products), not to PATIENTS as people. Refusing to treat a patient based on their identity is discrimination, not conscientious objection.

---

### Operational Debrief 3

- **Why B is correct:** The ethical framework distinguishes between objecting to a PROCEDURE (protected) and objecting to a PATIENT (discrimination). Wound care doesn't conflict with any religious belief — only the patient's identity does, and that's discrimination.
- **Why A is incorrect:** Conscientious objection protects you from performing specific acts that violate your beliefs (e.g., participation in abortion, administering blood transfusions). It does NOT entitle you to refuse care to entire categories of people based on who they are.
- **Why C is boundary violation:** Sharing unsolicited religious perspectives with a patient is proselytizing, which violates professional boundaries and the patient's right to respectful treatment.
- **Why D is discriminatory documentation:** Documenting personal objections to a patient's identity in their medical record is both inappropriate and potentially actionable.
- **Legal Reality:** Refusing to care for a patient based on protected characteristics (sexual orientation) = potential discrimination claim, termination, and licensure action.
- **Patient Impact:** Patients who sense judgment or bias from clinicians withhold information → care quality suffers → adverse events increase.

---

## 6. Summary & Synthesis

**Key Takeaways:**
1. Ethical ≠ Legal. You must meet BOTH standards, and sometimes they conflict.
2. Advance directives are legally binding — family emotion does not override documented patient wishes.
3. The Ethics Committee exists for YOU — use it when you face genuine dilemmas.
4. Conscientious objection = refusing specific PROCEDURES, not refusing PATIENTS.
5. Patient autonomy means you respect their decisions even when you disagree — including "bad" decisions.
6. Document ethical reasoning, not just actions — your defensibility depends on showing you thought it through.

**Operational Bridge:** Your field preceptor will evaluate your ability to navigate advance directive situations, maintain professional boundaries, manage value conflicts without patient impact, and know when to escalate to the Ethics Committee.

### Exam Questions

**Q1.** Healthcare ethics involves:
- [ ] A. Following the law exactly
- [ ] B. Doing what feels right personally
- [x] C. Making well-researched, considerate decisions that account for patient beliefs, wishes, and welfare alongside medical best practices
- [ ] D. Deferring all decisions to the physician

**Q2.** A valid advance directive can be overridden by:
- [ ] A. A family member's emotional request
- [ ] B. The nurse's clinical judgment
- [x] C. A court order (in extreme circumstances)
- [ ] D. The physician's preference

**Q3.** A staff member's right to conscientious objection applies to:
- [ ] A. Refusing to care for patients they personally dislike
- [x] B. Declining participation in specific procedures that conflict with religious/moral beliefs, provided patient care is not disrupted
- [ ] C. Choosing which patients to see based on personal preferences
- [ ] D. Refusing mandatory training that conflicts with beliefs

**Q4.** The Ethics Committee should be consulted when:
- [x] A. A genuine ethical dilemma exists where multiple valid principles conflict
- [ ] B. A patient is difficult
- [ ] C. A staff member disagrees with a supervisor
- [ ] D. Only when a physician requests it

**Q5.** A patient with advanced cancer tells you: "Don't tell my family how bad it is — they'll put me in a nursing home." The physician has been informing the family against the patient's wishes. This is:
- [ ] A. Acceptable — families need to know
- [x] B. A potential ethical violation — the competent patient has the right to control who receives their health information
- [ ] C. The physician's decision to make
- [ ] D. Not your concern as a nurse

**Q6.** Professional boundaries in healthcare mean:
- [x] A. No sexual relationships with patients; no financial entanglement; maintaining the therapeutic relationship only
- [ ] B. Being cold and distant
- [ ] C. Never showing compassion
- [ ] D. Only applying to physicians

**Q7.** A patient refuses physical therapy but wants to continue receiving nursing visits. The CORRECT response is:
- [ ] A. Discharge the patient for non-compliance
- [x] B. Respect the partial refusal — patients can accept some services while declining others
- [ ] C. Require the patient to accept all ordered services or none
- [ ] D. Override the refusal since the physician ordered it

**Q8.** The Agency's Code of Ethics covers: (Select ALL that apply)
- [x] A. Patient Rights and Responsibilities
- [x] B. Relationships to Other Provider Agencies
- [x] C. Fiscal Responsibilities
- [x] D. Marketing and Public Relations
- [x] E. Personnel

**Q9.** When a patient's family is concealing the patient's true condition from them (with physician participation), this raises ethical concerns about:
- [x] A. The patient's right to honest information and informed decision-making
- [ ] B. The family's right to protect the patient
- [ ] C. Nothing — the physician authorized it
- [ ] D. Only HIPAA compliance

**Q10.** Suspected drug activity in a patient's home that threatens your safety should be:
- [ ] A. Ignored — you're there for patient care
- [ ] B. Reported to law enforcement by you directly
- [x] C. Reported to your supervisor; leave if immediate danger exists; Ethics Committee/administration determines appropriate next steps
- [ ] D. Documented in the patient's chart as "non-compliant environment"

## 8. Mandatory Final Operational Debrief

**Reflection Prompt:** "A correct answer does NOT guarantee correct reasoning."

1. Ethics are not just for dramatic situations — they govern every clinical interaction.
2. Your personal beliefs are VALID but they NEVER override patient rights or professional duty.
3. The Ethics Committee is a STRENGTH to use, not a weakness to avoid.
4. Advance directives represent the patient speaking — honor their voice even when others are louder.
5. Documentation of ethical reasoning is your defensibility — "I did the right thing" is not proof.
