## 1. Module Overview

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) governs how patient health information is created, stored, transmitted, and disclosed. Field workers handle Protected Health Information (PHI) in uncontrolled environments — patient homes, vehicles, public spaces — creating unique risks. This module ensures clinicians understand privacy obligations, the minimum necessary rule, patient consent/authorization distinctions, and their personal liability for violations.

## 2. Pre-Assessment Hook (Scenario-Based)

**Scenario:** You are finishing documentation in your car outside a patient's apartment complex. A neighbor approaches your window and says: "I noticed you visiting Mrs. Johnson — is she okay? We haven't seen her out walking in weeks. I'm her friend from church and I'm worried." Mrs. Johnson has not signed any authorization for this person.

**Question:** What is your CORRECT response?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Tell the neighbor Mrs. Johnson is doing fine to ease their worry — no medical details are shared |
| B | Confirm you visit Mrs. Johnson but explain you cannot discuss her condition without her written authorization |
| C | State that you cannot confirm or deny whether you are providing services to anyone at this address |
| D | Give the neighbor the office phone number so they can call and be connected to Mrs. Johnson |

**Correct:** C  
**Hook Purpose:** Even confirming that someone is a patient constitutes disclosure of PHI. Most learners will choose B, which still violates HIPAA by confirming the care relationship.

---

## 3. Learning Objectives

Upon completion, the learner will be able to:

1. Define Protected Health Information (PHI) and Individually Identifiable Health Information (IIHI).
2. Identify all "covered entities" under HIPAA and their obligations.
3. Distinguish between patient consent (treatment/payment) and authorization (other uses).
4. Apply the "minimum necessary rule" in clinical and documentation contexts.
5. Enumerate specific patient rights under HIPAA privacy regulations.
6. Describe situations where PHI may be disclosed without patient consent (public health, law enforcement, emergencies).

---

## 4. Core Concepts (Terminology) — Max 7

| # | Term | Definition | Operational Meaning | Why It Matters |
|---|------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Protected Health Information (PHI) | All information created by any covered entity in any form (paper, electronic, verbal, photo, video) | Everything you document, say, photograph, or transmit about a patient | Unauthorized disclosure = federal penalties + termination |
| 2 | Individually Identifiable Health Information (IIHI) | Information reasonably linkable to a specific patient (name, DOB, SSN, address, MRN, photos) | Even zip codes (<20K population) or partial dates can identify someone | De-identification requires removing ALL 18 identifiers |
| 3 | Minimum Necessary Rule | Only the minimum information needed may be shared, even with authorization | Don't send entire chart when a summary suffices | Over-sharing = violation even when authorization exists |
| 4 | Covered Entity | Healthcare providers, health plans, clearinghouses, and business associates | YOU are a covered entity; violations attach to individuals | Personal criminal liability possible for willful violations |
| 5 | Privacy Officer | Designated person who monitors and audits HIPAA compliance | The Administrator in this Agency | All violations reported to this person; complaints investigated internally |
| 6 | Informed Consent | Signed at first encounter; covers treatment, payment, healthcare operations | Different from Authorization — covers routine use only | Must be carefully explained to patient; revocable |
| 7 | Authorization | Required for uses OUTSIDE treatment/payment/operations | Research, marketing, employer requests, legal proceedings | Patient entitled to free accounting of disclosures every 12 months |

## 5. Lesson Structure

### Lesson 1: HIPAA Fundamentals & Covered Entities

**Content Coverage:**
- HIPAA legislative history and purpose (1996)
- Components: portability, administrative simplification, privacy, security
- Covered entities: providers, health plans, clearinghouses, business associates
- State law supremacy when more stringent than HIPAA
- PHI definition: all forms (paper, electronic, verbal, video, photos, computer screens, internet)
- IIHI: 18 identifiers that make information individually identifiable

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- HIPAA compliance is MANDATORY, not voluntary
- If state law is MORE strict than HIPAA → follow state law
- You are personally a covered entity — not just the Agency
- PHI includes what you SAY verbally, not just what's written
- Even looking at a chart of a patient NOT under your care = violation

---

### Challenge 1: PHI Identification

**Scenario:** You are documenting a complex wound care visit in your car. You receive a text from a coworker: "Hey, how's the patient on Oak Street doing? The one with the Stage 4 pressure ulcer? I had her last week." You know which patient they mean.

**Question:** What is the CORRECT response?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Reply with a brief clinical update since the coworker had the patient last week |
| B | Reply "She's doing better" without specifics — that's not really PHI |
| C | Do not respond with any patient information via unsecured text; direct the coworker to access the clinical record through proper channels |
| D | Call the coworker back and give a verbal update since texts leave a trail |

**Correct:** C  
**Rationale:** Unsecured text messages are not an approved communication channel for PHI. Even confirmation of who the patient is combined with clinical status violates minimum necessary and transmission security requirements.

---

### Operational Debrief 1

- **Why C is correct:** PHI must only be transmitted through secure, agency-approved channels. The coworker can access the chart through proper systems.
- **Why A is a violation:** Unsecured text + clinical detail + identifying information (address + diagnosis) = clear HIPAA breach.
- **Why B is still a violation:** "She's doing better" confirms identity (Oak Street + pressure ulcer context) + provides clinical status via unsecured channel.
- **Why D doesn't fix it:** The issue isn't the medium's traceability — it's that verbal communication in an uncontrolled setting (car, potentially overheard) with unnecessary detail violates minimum necessary.
- **Workflow Impact:** Develop the habit of ALWAYS directing clinical questions to secured systems.
- **Patient Safety Impact:** Data breaches erode patient trust → patients withhold information → care quality suffers.
- **Survey Implication:** ACHC evaluates communication security practices during surveys.

---

### Lesson 2: Patient Rights Under HIPAA

**Content Coverage:**
- Right to privacy notice
- Right to request restrictions on disclosure
- Right to access PHI (inspect and copy)
- Right to know what disclosures have been made (accounting)
- Right to amend PHI
- Informed consent vs. authorization distinction
- Information Practices document requirements
- Revocation of consent process

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Patients can RESTRICT who receives their information (even family members)
- Patients can REVOKE consent at any time
- Patients get a free accounting of disclosures every 12 months
- Consent covers treatment/payment/operations; ANYTHING ELSE requires authorization
- Patients can amend their PHI — agency may deny but must document

---

### Challenge 2: Patient Rights Application

**Scenario:** A patient's adult daughter calls you during your drive between visits: "I'm listed as my mother's emergency contact. I need to know what medications she's on because I'm picking up her prescriptions." The patient is cognitively intact and has not listed the daughter on any HIPAA authorization form.

**Question:** What is the CORRECT action?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Provide the medication list — she's the emergency contact, which implies authorization |
| B | Decline to share information; explain that emergency contact status does not equal HIPAA authorization; direct her to discuss with her mother or the office |
| C | Give only medication names but not dosages — that's partial information, not a full disclosure |
| D | Tell the daughter to come to the next visit so the patient can give verbal consent |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** Emergency contact designation does NOT constitute HIPAA authorization. The patient is cognitively intact and must independently authorize disclosure to any party.

---

### Operational Debrief 2

- **Why B is correct:** No authorization on file + cognitively intact patient = NO disclosure. Period. The daughter's relationship or emergency contact status is irrelevant.
- **Why A is a violation:** Emergency contact ≠ HIPAA authorization. This is one of the most common real-world HIPAA breaches.
- **Why C is still a violation:** Partial PHI is still PHI. Medication names are protected health information regardless of whether dosages are included.
- **Why D is problematic:** While well-intentioned, it creates a delay that may compromise care (prescriptions need filling) without addressing the real solution: have the patient complete a proper authorization form.
- **Escalation Path:** Inform the office; have care coordinator assist the patient with authorization paperwork if she wishes.
- **Legal Impact:** Unauthorized disclosure to family = reportable breach. Even one instance can trigger OCR investigation.

---

### Lesson 3: Permitted Disclosures & Practical Safeguards

**Content Coverage:**
- Situations where PHI can be shared WITHOUT consent (emergency 911, communicable disease, law enforcement, national security, public health reporting)
- Public health reporting requirements (death, communicable disease, child abuse, adverse drug reactions, cancer registry, meningitis, immunizations)
- Practical safeguards: sign-in sheets, calling patients by name, room identifiers
- Clergy visits and patient right to restrict
- One-call rule for organ procurement
- Administrative safeguards: privacy officer, compliance process, training, sanctions, anti-retaliation

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- You CAN disclose in genuine emergencies without prior consent
- Reportable communicable diseases MUST be reported to public health
- Sign-in sheets OK (name + time only, no appointment reason)
- Patients can be called by name to treatment areas
- Patient can restrict ALL information to visitors/callers (including hospital acknowledgment)
- All HIPAA violations must be documented; sanctions parallel other disciplinary policies

### Challenge 3: Permitted Disclosure Decision

**Scenario:** During a routine home visit, you discover your patient has active, untreated tuberculosis. She begs you: "Please don't tell anyone — my family will be terrified and my neighbors will shun me." She refuses treatment referral.

**Question:** What is your LEGAL obligation?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Respect her wishes — patient autonomy and HIPAA prohibit disclosure without consent |
| B | Report to the local health department as required by law; explain to the patient that communicable diseases are mandatory exceptions to privacy protections |
| C | Only report if the patient has documented exposure to others |
| D | Wait to see if she changes her mind at the next visit |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** Communicable diseases are specifically identified as permitted/mandatory disclosures under HIPAA. Public health reporting supersedes patient consent requirements.

---

### Operational Debrief 3

- **Why B is correct:** HIPAA explicitly permits — and state law typically MANDATES — reporting of communicable diseases to public health. This is not a violation of the patient's rights.
- **Why A is dangerously wrong:** Patient autonomy does not extend to creating public health threats. Active TB is airborne and affects household members, neighbors, and clinicians.
- **Why C is wrong:** Mandatory reporting is triggered by confirmed/suspected diagnosis, not by documented exposure to specific individuals.
- **Why D is negligent:** Delay in reporting active TB = continued transmission risk + personal liability for the clinician.
- **Patient Safety Impact:** Every day of delay = potential new infections in household and community.
- **Legal Protection:** Mandatory reporters who report in good faith are protected from liability, even if the patient objects.

---

## 6. Summary & Synthesis

**Key Takeaways:**
1. PHI = ALL information in ALL forms (paper, electronic, verbal, visual) about a patient.
2. Even confirming someone IS a patient constitutes disclosure.
3. Emergency contact ≠ HIPAA authorization. Relationship ≠ authorization.
4. Minimum necessary rule applies EVEN WHEN authorization exists.
5. Mandatory reporting (TB, abuse, communicable diseases) overrides patient consent.
6. You are personally liable — not just the agency.

**Operational Bridge:** Your field preceptor will evaluate your handling of PHI in the field (car documentation, phone calls, device security), your response to family information requests, and your understanding of when disclosure IS permitted.

### Exam Questions

**Q1.** Which of the following is NOT considered Individually Identifiable Health Information?
- [ ] A. Social Security Number
- [ ] B. Date of birth
- [x] C. Gender (when reported alone without other identifiers)
- [ ] D. Medical record number

**Q2.** A patient's spouse demands to see the clinical record. The patient is conscious and has NOT authorized the spouse. You should:
- [ ] A. Show the record — spouses have inherent rights
- [x] B. Decline and explain that only the patient can authorize access
- [ ] C. Show only non-medical sections
- [ ] D. Have the spouse sign a witness form and then provide access

**Q3.** The "minimum necessary rule" means:
- [ ] A. Patients must be given the minimum necessary care
- [ ] B. Documentation should be as brief as possible
- [x] C. Only the minimum amount of PHI needed for the intended purpose may be shared
- [ ] D. HIPAA requirements are the minimum necessary for compliance

**Q4.** You accidentally send a patient's lab results to the wrong fax number. Your FIRST action is:
- [ ] A. Hope no one reads it and say nothing
- [x] B. Report the breach immediately to the Privacy Officer and document the incident
- [ ] C. Call the number and ask them to destroy it
- [ ] D. Resend to the correct number — the first send will be ignored

**Q5.** Which of the following may be disclosed WITHOUT patient consent? (Select ALL that apply)
- [x] A. Communicable disease report to public health department
- [x] B. Information to law enforcement at a crime scene via 911

**Q6.** HIPAA requires that patient consent is:
- [ ] A. Signed only for hospital admissions

**Q7.** A researcher asks for access to patient charts for a diabetes study. This requires:
- [ ] A. Only verbal patient consent
- [x] B. Written patient authorization AND IRB-approved research protocol

**Q8.** The Privacy Officer for this Agency is the:
- [x] A. Administrator

**Q9.** A patient requests an amendment to their medical record. The Agency:
- [ ] A. Must always approve the amendment

**Q10.** You leave a patient's paper chart visible on your car seat while getting gas. A passerby photographs it through the window. This is:
- [ ] A. Not a violation because you didn't intentionally share it
- [ ] B. The passerby's violation, not yours
- [x] C. A HIPAA violation by you for failing to safeguard PHI

## 8. Mandatory Final Operational Debrief

**Reflection Prompt:** "A correct answer does NOT guarantee correct reasoning."

1. HIPAA violations don't require INTENT — negligence is sufficient for liability.
2. The field environment (cars, homes, public spaces) creates MORE risk than office settings.
3. Verbal disclosures are violations too — not just written/electronic ones.
4. "But they're family" is NEVER a defense for unauthorized disclosure.
5. When in doubt, DON'T disclose. Check with your Privacy Officer first.
