## 1. Module Overview

Home health field workers operate independently in patient homes during emergencies when communication systems, transportation, and normal support structures may be compromised. This module prepares clinicians to execute the Agency's Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan, triage patients by acuity classification, and maintain continuity of care under disaster conditions.

---

## 2. Pre-Assessment Hook (Scenario-Based)

**Scenario:** A wildfire is approaching the area where 3 of your assigned patients live. Your cell service is intermittent. Patient A is on a ventilator with no backup power. Patient B is ambulatory but confused (dementia) and lives alone. Patient C has a family caregiver and stable chronic conditions. You have 45 minutes before evacuation orders take effect.

**Question:** What is your PRIORITY action sequence?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Visit all three patients in geographic order to save drive time |
| B | Call 911 for Patient A (life-sustaining equipment), then go to Patient B (vulnerable/alone), then call Patient C's caregiver with instructions |
| C | Go to the office for instructions before visiting anyone |
| D | Call all three patients and tell them to evacuate on their own |

**Correct:** B  
**Hook Purpose:** Exposes the learner's ability to triage under pressure using the Agency's emergency classification system (Class I–IV) before the content is taught.

---

## 3. Learning Objectives

Upon completion, the learner will be able to:

1. Describe the Agency's Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan structure and their role within it.
2. Classify patients using the 4-tier emergency triage system (Class I–IV).
3. Identify the three categories of emergencies (Man-Made, Natural, Technological).
4. Execute personal and automobile preparedness requirements for field staff.
5. Apply the correct communication and escalation protocols when normal systems are compromised.
6. Describe documentation requirements during and after an emergency event.

---

## 4. Core Concepts (Terminology) — Max 7

| # | Term | Definition | Operational Meaning | Why It Matters |
|---|------|-----------|-------------------|---------------|
| 1 | Class I Emergency | Life-threatening condition requiring life-sustaining medical devices | Patient must be seen IMMEDIATELY; coordinate evacuation to acute care | Failure = potential patient death; highest liability |
| 2 | Class II Emergency | Condition requiring nursing intervention within 24–48 hours | Prioritized after Class I; rescheduling required | Delayed care may result in hospitalization |
| 3 | Class III Emergency | Stable condition; services can be postponed 48–72 hours | Can be managed by phone with patient/caregiver | Over-triaging wastes resources during emergencies |
| 4 | Class IV Emergency | Stable, independent patient; services postponable >72 hours | Lowest priority; notify of delay only | Under-triaging a Class I as Class IV = negligence |
| 5 | Continuity of Operations Plan | Business continuity plan addressing financial needs, essential functions, and return to normal | Agency stays functional during disaster; you remain paid | CMS CoP requires documented continuity planning |
| 6 | Disaster Coordinator | Agency Administrator (or designee) responsible for activating the emergency plan | Single point of authority for all emergency decisions | Chain of command must be clear; freelancing is prohibited |
| 7 | Mitigation/Preparedness/Response/Recovery | Four phases of emergency planning | Each phase has distinct staff responsibilities | Annual drill required; ACHC surveys for compliance |

## 5. Lesson Structure

### Lesson 1: Emergency Preparedness Plan Framework

**Content Coverage:**
- Agency Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan overview
- Roles: Administrator as Disaster Coordinator, Alternate Administrator
- 13-point compliance framework (risk assessment, communication procedures, patient categorization)
- Annual review requirements and Professional Advisory Committee role
- Documentation of compliance requirements

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Agency is NOT required to physically evacuate or transport patients
- ALL employees must be oriented to the plan and their specific responsibilities
- Plan is reviewed yearly AND after every emergency response event
- Patient emergency contacts and triage categories must be readily accessible

---

### Challenge 1: Plan Activation Scenario

**Scenario:** It's 2:00 AM on a Saturday. You receive a weather alert on your phone about flash flooding in your service area. You have 6 patients scheduled for morning visits. Your office is closed and you cannot reach the Administrator.

**Question:** According to Agency policy, what should you do FIRST?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Cancel all visits and go back to sleep — the office will handle it Monday |
| B | Attempt contact with the Alternate Administrator/on-call supervisor using the established communication chain |
| C | Drive to each patient's home to check on them personally |
| D | Post on social media asking if anyone knows what to do |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** The communication plan has primary AND alternate contact modes. Field staff must follow the chain of command, not act independently or ignore the situation.

---

### Operational Debrief 1

- **Why B is correct:** The plan designates alternate contacts for exactly this situation. After-hours emergencies have a defined escalation path.
- **Why A is dangerous:** Ignoring a potential Class I/II situation until Monday could result in patient death. Field staff have monitoring responsibilities.
- **Why C is reckless:** Driving into a flood zone without coordination puts you at risk, removes you from communication, and violates the plan's coordinated approach.
- **Why D is absurd:** Social media is never an appropriate emergency communication tool for patient care decisions.
- **Workflow Impact:** The plan specifies after-hours monitoring responsibilities for all staff — this isn't optional.
- **Patient Safety Impact:** Delayed triage during weather events = preventable adverse events.
- **Survey Implication:** ACHC verifies that staff know how to activate the plan, not just that the plan exists.

---

### Lesson 2: Patient Triage Classification System

**Content Coverage:**
- Class I–IV Emergency classifications with clinical examples
- Factors for prioritization when demand exceeds resources
- Patient categorization by acuity, continuity needs, and availability of alternative caregivers
- Special populations: ventilator-dependent, infusion patients, multiple assistive devices
- Evacuation assistance identification for local/state coordination

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Classification is based on: medical acuity + availability of alternative support + usual hours of service
- Patients with NO alternative caregiver receive HIGHER priority than those with family support
- Classification must be pre-assigned and accessible in emergencies, not determined in real-time
- Agency identifies in advance which patients may need evacuation assistance

---

### Challenge 2: Triage Decision

**Scenario:** During a power outage expected to last 12+ hours, you are triaging your caseload:
- Patient 1: On home ventilator, spouse present, backup battery lasts 4 hours
- Patient 2: Diabetic on insulin pump, lives alone, no backup supplies
- Patient 3: Post-surgical wound care, daughter visiting, stable vital signs
- Patient 4: Weekly PT exercises, independent, just saw physician yesterday

**Question:** Rank these patients from HIGHEST to LOWEST priority:

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| B | 2, 1, 3, 4 |
| C | 1, 3, 2, 4 |
| D | 2, 1, 4, 3 |

**Correct:** A  
**Rationale:** Patient 1 is Class I (life-sustaining device with limited backup). Patient 2 is Class I/II (device-dependent, alone). Patient 3 is Class II/III (stable with caregiver present). Patient 4 is Class IV (stable, independent, recently seen).

---

### Operational Debrief 2

- **Why A is correct:** Ventilator + limited battery = most immediate threat to life. Insulin pump patient alone is next because they have NO alternative caregiver. Spouse presence for Patient 1 doesn't reduce classification — the device dependency does.
- **Why B is wrong:** Although Patient 2 is alone, ventilator failure is more immediately life-threatening than insulin pump failure.
- **Why C is wrong:** Patient 3 has a caregiver present AND is stable — lower priority than the device-dependent patient alone.
- **Why D is wrong:** Patient 4 (Class IV) can never rank above Patient 3 (Class II/III).
- **Documentation Impact:** Triage decisions must be documented with rationale, time, and actions taken.
- **Reimbursement Impact:** Emergency visits must be documented per emergency protocols to support billing.

---

### Lesson 3: Emergency Types & Personal Preparedness

**Content Coverage:**
- Three categories: Man-Made, Natural, Technological emergencies
- Staff Emergency Preparedness Plan requirements
- Automobile preparedness (supplies, fuel, maps, emergency kit)
- Family preparedness requirements for field staff
- Damage of Written Records procedures
- Communication when normal systems fail (alternate modes)

**Key Operational Takeaways:**
- Field staff must maintain personal/vehicle emergency preparedness year-round
- Family plan must be established so staff can respond without personal distraction
- Paper records damage protocol: document attempts to comply even when unable
- Agency cannot be required to provide care when roads are impassable or patient location is unknown

---

### Challenge 3: Post-Emergency Documentation

**Scenario:** After an earthquake, you were unable to reach 2 of your 5 patients for 3 days. Roads were impassable. You had no cell service for 36 hours. When service resumed, you learned both patients were safe with neighbors. Your supervisor asks for documentation of the event.

**Question:** What MUST your documentation include?

| Option | Text |
|--------|------|
| A | Just a note saying "earthquake prevented visits" |
| B | Detailed record of: dates/times of attempted contact, methods used, barriers encountered, when contact was restored, patient status upon contact, and actions taken |
| C | Nothing — the agency's general disaster log covers everyone |
| D | A signed statement from the patients that they weren't harmed |

**Correct:** B  
**Rationale:** The Agency must document attempts to follow procedures even when unable to comply. Individual-level documentation protects both staff and agency.

---

### Operational Debrief 3

- **Why B is correct:** CMS and ACHC require documentation of good-faith attempts to provide care. "Best efforts" must be provable.
- **Why A is insufficient:** Lacks specificity required for survey defense. No timestamps, no barrier documentation, no resolution.
- **Why C is wrong:** Individual patient records must reflect individual-level events, not just aggregate disaster logs.
- **Why D is irrelevant:** Patient statements about non-harm don't address the regulatory requirement to document care continuity efforts.
- **Survey Implication:** ACHC will review post-emergency documentation during surveys to verify plan execution.
- **Legal Protection:** Detailed documentation of inability to comply (force majeure) is the agency's legal shield.

---

## 6. Summary & Synthesis

**Key Takeaways:**
1. The Agency's Emergency Plan has 4 phases: Mitigation, Preparedness, Response, Recovery — each with distinct staff duties.
2. Patient triage uses Classes I–IV; classification is pre-assigned, not improvised during the emergency.
3. Three factors drive priority: medical acuity, alternative caregiver availability, usual hours of service.
4. Field staff must maintain personal, vehicle, and family preparedness at all times.
5. Agency is NOT required to provide care when physically impossible — but MUST document attempts.
6. Annual drills are required; knowledge of the plan is verified by ACHC surveyors.

**Operational Bridge:** Your field preceptor will verify that you can locate your assigned patients' emergency classifications, articulate the communication chain, and demonstrate your vehicle emergency kit.

## 8. Mandatory Final Operational Debrief

**Reflection Prompt:** "A correct answer does NOT guarantee correct reasoning."

1. Emergency preparedness is a CONTINUOUS state — not something you activate only when a disaster occurs.
2. Triage decisions are pre-planned and evidence-based, not emotional or geographic.
3. Documentation during emergencies is MORE important, not less — it's your legal and regulatory defense.
4. Communication chain failures have backup modes for a reason — know them before you need them.
5. "Best efforts" under impossible conditions is acceptable; NO effort is never acceptable.

**Confidence Slider:** "How confident are you in executing your role in the Agency Emergency Plan without supervisor guidance?" (1–5 scale)
