## Scene 5 — What Makes Home Health Different: "The Debrief After the Shadow Visit"

### Learner Role

### Field Notes
**You Are a Guest in Their Home** — In a facility, the room belongs to the building. In a patient's home, the home belongs to the patient. This is a workplace-environment point, not a codified patient-rights matter: Alex's job is to adjust to their routines, their space, and their pace — not ask them to adjust to a clinical setting — because that's how the workspace actually operates, not because a specific regulation requires it in this scene. If something in the home looks unsafe, Alex's move is to note what was observed and loop in Dana the same day, rather than rearrange or repair anything personally.

**You Practice with Real Autonomy** — There's no nurse down the hall and no physician five minutes away. Every assessment decision in the field is Alex's own clinical judgment, made in real time. That doesn't mean improvising when something is unclear — it means knowing exactly which situations require a call to the supervisor before Alex moves forward.

**Documentation Is Your Defense** — On a hospital floor, three other people might have seen what Alex saw. In a patient's home, Alex is often the only professional who was there. The note Alex writes afterward isn't paperwork — it's the entire record of what happened, and it has to stand on its own.

**The Patient's Home Is Your Workplace** — Alex doesn't get to choose the conditions of a visit — pets, opinionated relatives, clutter, or safety hazards all come with the address. Alex can't rebuild someone's living room, but Alex can always put the observation in the chart and request a safety intervention through the proper channel.

**When You Can't Reach Someone in the Hallway: Escalating and Coordinating Remotely** — In a facility, Alex could catch the PT in the hallway. In home health, the team is scattered across the county, so reaching a teammate for routine coordination takes an intentional call, message, or note the same day. But routine coordination is not the same skill as escalation: if what Alex finds crosses from "the team should know" into "someone needs to act now," the response changes — Alex calls Dana or Compliance directly and without delay, because at that point it is no longer a coordination task, it's an escalation.

### Reference Notes
- Reference: 42 CFR §484.110 — Conditions of Participation: Clinical Records. Governs documentation completeness and retention; supports why home health documentation must stand alone as the record. Cited only for the Documentation Is Your Defense node. Informational.
- Reference: ACHC Home Health Standards — home visits occur without on-site clinical backup; supervisory availability is by phone/escalation, not physical presence. Informational.
- Reference: Care Indeed care-coordination protocol — interdisciplinary communication (RN/PT/MSW) is documented and routed through agency systems, not informal hallway contact; the same protocol also defines when routine coordination must instead be treated as an escalation. Informational.
- Note: This scene cites §484.110 (Clinical Records) for the documentation node only. It does not cite, and must never cite, §484.50 (Patient Rights) anywhere in this scene. The "Guest in the Home" node is deliberately framed above as an operational/workplace-environment point (how the workspace is structured and who adapts to whom) rather than as dignity, respect, informed participation, or refusal-rights content — that patient-rights framing under §484.50 is reserved for Scene 7, and its vocabulary (dignity, respect, rights) is intentionally absent from this node's Field Note and narration. §484.110 must never be paired with dignity/respect language, and this scene's only two node-adjacent concepts that could be mistaken for dignity content (Guest in the Home; The Patient's Home Is Your Workplace) are both written in operational/logistics terms for that reason.
