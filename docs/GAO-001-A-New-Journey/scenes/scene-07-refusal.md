## Scene 7 — Scenario Challenge 1, Patient Refusal: "The Right to Say No"

### Story Beat
It's Thursday. Alex is now doing supervised solo-style visits, and today's stop is Mr. Ray Torres — the same composite patient from the source inventory's Scenario 1, but this scene is a new, distinct moment: before the bruising observation ever comes up, Mr. Torres flatly refuses a wound-care teaching task Alex has come to deliver. Alex's SNF instinct is to keep pushing gently until the patient agrees; that instinct is wrong here. This scene tests whether Alex can hold two things at once — respect for Mr. Torres's right to decide, and the professional duty to make sure that decision is genuinely informed. Continuity: this is the same pause-and-observe discipline Alex has been building all week — noticing what a situation actually calls for before acting, rather than defaulting to habit — and it primes the composure Alex will need moments later in this same visit when the bruising is noticed (Scene 8, via the mandatory-reporting flow). Dana is not physically present — this is Alex acting on training, which is the point of the week.

### Learner Role
The learner acts as Alex during the refusal moment: choosing how to respond when Mr. Torres declines the wound-care teaching, choosing what to explain and how, deciding whether/how to notify the care team, and deciding what belongs in the visit note. Every choice is consequential — there is no passive "click to continue" step in the five nodes; each requires picking the professionally correct action from plausible-but-wrong alternatives.

### Field Notes
**Respect the Refusal** — Mr. Torres has the right to decline any treatment or teaching, even when Alex believes it's a good idea. Assuming he lacks capacity, or treating the refusal as a problem to solve, is not the correct read here — the correct first move is calm acknowledgment, not persuasion.

**Explain the Risk** — This note carries the documentation angle, distinct from the node-unlock narration's explanation of *what* to say: whatever Alex explains here has to show up later in the visit note in the same plain language, not translated into clinical shorthand after the fact. If the note says "risks explained" with nothing else, it reads exactly like the conversation never happened — the record has to carry the same specificity as the conversation itself.

**Notify Appropriately** — A patient's refusal is routine clinical information, not an emergency. Alex tells the supervising RN the same day through the normal reporting channel — this keeps the care team aligned without treating a personal choice as a crisis.

**Document the Response** — Alex's note states only what happened: the risks explained, Mr. Torres's own words declining, and that the decision was respected. No guesses about why he refused, no editorializing — just the facts, the same discipline from the Field Notebook habit.

**Continue Safely** — One declined task doesn't end the visit. Alex completes the rest of the plan of care Mr. Torres has agreed to, because a refusal of one thing is not a refusal of everything.

### Reference Notes
Reference: 42 CFR §484.50 — Conditions of Participation: Patient Rights. Covers dignity, informed participation, and the right to refuse treatment. Informational; see policy for the governing requirement.

Reference: A surveyor may ask how staff document a patient's refusal of care. Informational — the expected answer is "explain risks, document objectively, notify the care team same day."

*(§484.110 is intentionally not cited in this scene — documentation duty here is a consequence of the refusal, not a rights citation; keeping the two concepts separate per the citation map.)*

### Narration Plan

**Scene-start** (story framing + concept intro, ~95 words):
"It's Thursday afternoon, and Alex is at Ray Torres's home for a wound-care visit — the kind of supervised, solo-style visit Alex has been building toward all week. Alex sets up to teach Mr. Torres how to change his own dressing between visits. Mr. Torres shakes his head. 'Not today. I don't want to do that.' In a hospital ward, another clinician might be down the hall to consult. Here, it's just Alex, the decision, and everything Dana has already taught about patient rights. Federal rule 42 CFR 484.50 gives Mr. Torres the right to refuse — the question is what Alex does next."

**Node-unlock — gao001-s7-respect-refusal** (~110 words):
"Alex's first instinct from the hospital floor is to keep offering, gently, until the patient comes around. That instinct doesn't belong here. Mr. Torres is alert, oriented, and has just made a clear decision about his own body and his own home — that's exactly what 42 CFR 484.50, the Patient Rights rule, protects: a patient's right to refuse treatment, free of pressure. It's the same instinct Alex practiced with Mr. Okafor — pause, observe, don't assume — just aimed at a different kind of moment. Alex isn't being told to give up on the teaching; Alex is being told that persuasion has a limit, and respect for the refusal comes first, not last. Nothing about a refusal makes Mr. Torres a difficult patient — it makes him a patient exercising a right the agency is required to honor."

**Node-unlock — gao001-s7-explain-risk** (~120 words):
"Respecting the refusal doesn't mean the conversation is over. Before Alex does anything else, Alex explains — in plain language, not clinical shorthand — what could happen if the dressing isn't changed correctly between visits: delayed healing, infection risk, a possible unplanned visit to the wound clinic. Alex also lays out the alternative: Care Indeed can adjust the visit schedule so a nurse changes the dressing more often instead. This is what makes a refusal an *informed* refusal, not just a rejected task. Dana's rule from the mission briefing applies directly here: patient-centered means starting with what the patient needs to decide well, not with what's easiest for the visit schedule. Explaining risk isn't arguing — it's making sure Mr. Torres has what he needs before Alex steps back."

**Node-unlock — gao001-s7-notify-appropriately** (~95 words):
"Once the conversation is done, Alex still owes the care team a heads-up — but this isn't a 911 moment and it isn't a mandatory-reporting trigger. It's routine clinical communication. Alex notifies the supervising RN the same day, through the normal channel, exactly the way Alex would report any other clinical finding from the visit. The care team needs to know Mr. Torres declined the teaching so the next visit's plan of care can account for it — not because anything went wrong, but because coordination is how the whole team stays aligned on one patient."

**Node-unlock — gao001-s7-document-response** (~100 words):
"Alex opens the field notebook and writes down exactly what happened — no interpretation, just facts: the risks and alternatives explained, Mr. Torres's own words declining the teaching, and the fact that the refusal was respected without pressure. This is the same discipline Alex has carried all week: in home health, this note is often the only record anyone will ever have of this conversation. If it isn't written down, plainly and objectively, it didn't happen — not because Alex doesn't trust their own memory, but because documentation is the defense every home health clinician relies on when nobody else was in the room."

**Node-unlock — gao001-s7-continue-safely** (~85 words):
"Mr. Torres declined one part of today's visit — he didn't decline all of it. Alex finishes the rest of the plan of care he's already agreed to: vitals, medication review, checking in on how he's managing at home. A refusal narrows today's visit; it doesn't end it, and it doesn't change the plan of care going forward unless Mr. Torres says so at a future visit. Alex leaves having done exactly what the job requires: informed, unhurried, and complete."

**Feedback — correct pick, any node** (~45 words, generic pattern, adapted per node): "That's the right call. [Node-specific one-clause reason, e.g., 'Acknowledging the refusal calmly, without pressure, is exactly what 484.50 protects.'] Alex moves forward with confidence, not second-guessing."

**Feedback — incorrect pick, respect-refusal node** (~70 words): "Continuing to press Mr. Torres — even gently — after he's said no isn't persistence, it's pressure, and 42 CFR 484.50 protects patients from exactly that. The correct move isn't to talk him into agreeing; it's to acknowledge the decision calmly and move to making sure it's an informed one. Try again — think about what respecting a refusal actually looks like in the room."

**Feedback — incorrect pick, explain-risk node (skip explanation)** (~65 words): "Skipping the risk conversation because Mr. Torres already said no isn't respect — it's a missed step. **Feedback — incorrect pick, notify-appropriately node (call 911/APS)** (~65 words): "Not here — nothing about this visit suggests abuse or neglect, so this isn't a mandatory-reporting situation. **Scene-complete** (~75 words): "Alex leaves Ray Torres's home having handled something that doesn't show up cleanly in a textbook: a patient saying no, and a clinician doing everything right anyway.
